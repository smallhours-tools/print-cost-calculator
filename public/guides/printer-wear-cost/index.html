3D printer wear and depreciation: what it costs per print hour

Every hour your printer runs uses up a little of the machine and its parts. That cost is real, but nobody can hand you the right number: printer makers don't publish a lifetime in hours, and how long a nozzle or build plate lasts depends on what you print. So this guide gives you the method and the arithmetic, and you supply the numbers. It takes about five minutes.

Wear has two parts: depreciation (the printer itself losing value) and maintenance (parts you replace). Add them to get a cost per print hour, then multiply by the print's hours.

Step 1: depreciation per print hour

Depreciation per hour = (price you paid − what you could sell it for later) ÷ print hours you expect before you replace it

For the hours, think in weeks rather than a lifetime: hours of printing per week × weeks you expect to keep the printer. If you print about 20 hours a week and upgrade every 3 years, that is 20 × 52 × 3 = 3,120 hours. If your printer or its app shows a total print time, use it to check your weekly figure.

Step 2: maintenance per print hour

Maintenance per hour = the sum of (part price ÷ print hours between replacements) for each part you actually replace: nozzles, build plates, belts, PTFE tubes, wipers, fans and so on.

The best source is your own history. Note the date and the printer's total print time each time you replace something; after a few months you have real intervals. Until then, a rough guess is fine, because these amounts are small. Here is the arithmetic with made-up numbers, only to show how it adds up:

Step 3: put it together

With the examples above, a printer with a net price of 400 over 3,120 hours costs 0.128 per hour, plus 0.042 maintenance: about 0.170 per print hour. For the 7 h 25 min print in our pricing guide, that is 0.95 of depreciation and 0.31 of maintenance, 1.26 in total: about as much as its 62 g of filament.

That is the point worth remembering: wear grows with print time, not with grams. A slow, light print (fine layers, small nozzle) can cost more in wear than in material, and pricing on grams alone misses it.

The calculator's defaults are placeholders

The calculator starts with a 400 printer over 4,000 hours (0.10 per hour) and 0.05 per hour of maintenance, so 0.15 per print hour. Those are round starting values, not measurements, and they are there so the wear line isn't zero. Replace them with your own figures from steps 1 and 2: the calculator has fields for printer price, lifetime hours and maintenance per hour, and remembers them in your browser.

Two things to leave out of wear: failed prints belong in the waste percentage (they cost filament and time, not machine life), and electricity has its own line (see electricity cost per print).
