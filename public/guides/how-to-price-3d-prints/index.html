How much should you charge for a 3D print?

Add up what the print really costs you (material, electricity, machine wear and your time), then set the price so that, after the marketplace takes its fee, the profit you wanted is still there. The second half is where most people lose money: cost plus 30% does not leave you 30% once fees come out of the price.

Step 1: what the print costs you

  • Material = grams ÷ 1000 × spool price per kg × (1 + waste %). Waste covers purge, supports you throw away and the occasional failed print. Your slicer reports the grams; see filament cost per gram for densities and per-gram prices.
  • Electricity = average watts × hours ÷ 1000 × price per kWh. Usually small; see electricity cost per print for measured watts by printer.
  • Printer wear = (purchase price ÷ the print hours you expect from the machine) × hours, plus a maintenance allowance per hour for nozzles, belts, build plates and the like. Both are your own estimates; there is no universal figure. See printer wear per print hour for how to work yours out.
  • Labor = your minutes per order (removing supports, packing, messages) × your hourly rate. Count only your hands-on time, not the hours the printer runs alone.

Step 2: margin and fees come out of the price

Marketplace fees are usually a percentage of the sale price plus a fixed amount per order, and a profit margin is a share of the price too. Both are taken from the price, so you divide instead of adding:

Price = (cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %)

With a 6.5% fee and a 30% margin you divide by 0.635. That looks steep, but it is exactly what makes 30% of the price land in your pocket.

Two things stand out. Labor is the biggest single cost, even at 10 minutes per order, and electricity is the smallest. In a case like this, knowing your real handling time matters far more than the exact electricity rate.

The common mistake: cost plus a markup

Take the same 5.03 cost and add 30%: the price is 6.54. The marketplace then takes 6.5% plus 0.25, which is 0.67, and your profit is 0.83, about 13% of the price rather than 30%, and a third of the 2.49 above. The gap grows with higher fees and on cheap items, where a fixed fee per order eats a larger share.

Checks before you list

  • Use the fee rates of your platform and payment processor. Check whether the percentage also applies to shipping, and whether any advertising fees apply to some sales.
  • Set waste from your own failure rate (see how to price in failed prints). Multicolor prints with purge towers can waste far more than 5%.
  • Price the batch, not one part. If you print 10 at once, labor and failures spread over 10 sales.
  • Compare with what similar items sell for. The formula gives your floor; the market sets the ceiling.
